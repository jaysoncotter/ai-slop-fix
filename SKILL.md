# AI Slop: A Catalog of Tells and Their Fixes

Slop is coverage without decisions: writing that touches every part of the request while committing to nothing a person who knew the subject would have committed to. It usually isn't wrong. It's unfalsifiable.

This is a flat catalog. Each entry names one tell, shows it, fixes it, and says when the same construction is fine. There is no hierarchy and nothing to classify. Find the tell, apply the fix, move on.

## Before flagging anything

Three questions, in order:

1. **Is the sentence doing a real job?** See [Things that look like tells and aren't](#things-that-look-like-tells-and-arent) at the bottom. Most false positives die here.
2. **Which clause could you delete without losing a fact?** If there is none, the sentence is clean regardless of how it sounds. If there is one, that clause is the finding.
3. **Does it recur?** One em dash, one hedge, one metaphor is a habit, not a finding. Flag what draws attention to itself, repeats, claims more than it supports, or slows a reader down.

Bad writing is not automatically slop. Awkward, ungrammatical, or blunt prose can be perfectly honest. Slop is specifically the polished kind that says nothing.

## The catalog

### Authority with no name

An appeal to people who are never identified.

- ✗ "Industry observers agree that warehouses adopting this approach see stronger throughput."
- ✓ "The 2025 operations review found the four warehouses on the new pick path averaged 340 units per hour, against 280 at the others."

**Fine when** the source is named and checkable, even loosely: "our own three-month pilot," "the vendor's published benchmark."

### Bolted-on benefit

A real sentence with an abstract reward stapled to the end, usually as `, ensuring / driving / enabling / unlocking [good thing]`. The most recognizable shape in the catalog.

- ✗ "The portal consolidates shift data, ensuring a seamless manager experience."
- ✓ "The portal pulls shift data from three systems, which cut schedule-building time from 40 minutes to 15."

**Fix by deletion first.** "The portal consolidates shift data" alone is already better than the original.

### Canned empathy

Feeling performed at volume instead of the specifics that would show you understand.

- ✗ "I completely understand how frustrating and overwhelming this outage must feel for your team."
- ✓ "The outage ran four hours and hit your two busiest locations. Here's what we changed."

### Colon verdict

A colon that introduces a pronouncement rather than a list.

- ✗ "The market is crowded: features are not enough; the launch has to prove results."
- ✓ "Three competitors shipped the same feature last quarter, so the launch has to show results in a customer's own data."

**Fine when** something is actually listed: "Spend data sits in four places: the ERP, the card system, contracts, and a shared drive."

### Dead conclusion

A closing line that restates what the document already proved, or asserts a benefit nothing above it earned.

- ✗ "In conclusion, these steps can help the organization achieve its goals."
- ✓ Cut it, or say what happens next: "The first two changes start Monday. The third needs board approval in October."

The marker phrase is not the problem. Strip "In conclusion" and the sentence is still dead weight. Conversely, if a real sentence sits under the phrase, only the phrase comes off.

### Elevating negation

Deflating an ordinary reading to reach for a grander one.

- ✗ "The maintenance log isn't just a record — it's the foundation of a safer fleet."
- ✓ "The maintenance log shows which vehicles are overdue for inspection."

**The test:** negation that makes a claim *smaller* is analysis. Negation that makes it *bigger* is slop.

- ✓ "The rebuild reduces manual reconciliation; it does not eliminate it." *(narrows)*
- ✓ "The leak is in the return line, not the pump." *(both sides observable)*
- ✗ "This is not a pricing problem. It is a credibility problem." *(both sides are opinions; nothing could settle it)*

### Floating pronoun

Connectives and causal words arranged around a referent that was never established. Reads like reasoning; isn't.

- ✗ "Demand pushes it; few sites have it; so it keeps growing."
- ✓ "Charger demand is growing fastest at the eight sites that already have three-phase power."

### Hedge stack

One hedge is caution. Four in a row is a refusal to say anything.

- ✗ "It may potentially be worth considering whether we could possibly revisit the pricing assumption."
- ✓ "We should revisit the pricing assumption before the fall catalog closes."

**Fine when** the hedge carries its reason: "We may need to extend the deadline, depending on whether the vendor delivers the file by Friday." The reason is what turns a hedge into a qualification.

### Idiom instead of a number

A figure of speech occupying the slot where the fact goes. The sentence stops being checkable.

- ✗ "The night shift is doing the heavy lifting on throughput."
- ✓ "The night shift produced 62 percent of units this quarter."
- ✗ "The new triage step really moves the needle on wait times."
- ✓ "The new triage step cut median wait from 41 minutes to 26."

### Invented jargon

Terms that were coined on the spot, not terms your reader's field actually uses.

- ✗ "The redesign establishes an integrated readiness framework that drives sustained organizational capability."
- ✓ "The redesign lets the support team close tickets without routing them to engineering."

**Fine when** the term is real and used precisely. "Unit economics," "cohort," "credit hold," and "subrogation" are vocabulary, not fog. Specialized is not the problem. Undefined and invented is.

### Label bullets that all say one thing

Bold labels are not the problem. Labels that decorate a single repeated predicate are.

- ✗ "**Speed:** matters to customers. **Accuracy:** matters to customers. **Cost:** matters to customers."
- ✓ "**Scope:** all 2025 orders. **Method:** random sample of 300. **Finding:** 9 percent shipped without a signed packing slip."

**The question:** does each label carry information the others don't?

### Metronome punctuation

Semicolons, colons, and dashes setting a beat instead of marking a relationship.

- ✗ "Budgets are set; risks are logged; approvals are granted — and the project moves ahead."
- ✓ "Finance sets the budget, the PMO logs risks, and the sponsor approves the launch."

Notice the fix does two things: ordinary commas replace the chain, and the passive verbs get actors. Frequency is what makes this a finding. One balanced semicolon in plain prose is nothing.

### Narrating your own output

The writer describing the thing the reader is about to read.

- ✗ "Below is a comprehensive and polished summary tailored to your needs."
- ✓ Delete it. Start with the summary.

### Process in place of a reason

Describing how a decision got made instead of why.

- ✗ "After several rounds of cross-functional review, we aligned on the revised staffing model."
- ✓ "We revised the staffing model because weekend coverage failed twice in July."

Watch for: *we aligned, we converged, the team came together, following extensive review.* A round of meetings is not a source. A source is something a reader could go look at.

### Runway

Scene-setting that has to be crossed before the document starts.

- ✗ "In today's rapidly evolving retail landscape, customer experience is more important than ever."
- ✓ Open on the finding: "Returns rose 14 percent after the checkout redesign."

**Fine when** the opener narrows scope, corrects the request, or explains an omission: "You asked for five bids; three arrived before the deadline, so this covers three."

### Sentence sprawl

A structure that would be fine in one clause, stretched across a colon into a semicolon chain.

- ✗ "The end state is therefore not 'one platform replaces the rest.' It is: shared records provide durable identity; the rules engine provides portable logic; each team becomes a view onto that shared graph."
- ✓ "Every team will read and write the same customer record instead of keeping its own copy."

The shape is fine. The sprawl isn't.

### Slogan triad

Three beats delivered for rhythm, usually as fragments.

- ✗ "Cleaner data. Faster reviews. Better decisions."
- ✓ "Deduplicating the vendor list cut review time from six days to two."

Related: the transition fragment, "From paper punch cards to a connected shop floor," which fixes to "Shift tracking moved from paper cards to the tablet app in March."

### Stripped condition

A claim with its *when* removed. This one runs backwards from the rest of the catalog: the fix is longer, not shorter.

- ✗ "Automating intake improves accuracy."
- ✓ "Automating intake improves accuracy when the source records are complete, and pushes rework downstream when they aren't."
- ✗ "The real question is where to draw the line."
- ✓ "The real question is where to draw the line on overtime before service quality drops."

A condition saying when a claim holds is the most substantive thing a sentence can carry. Never cut one in the name of concision.

### Superlative with nothing under it

- ✗ "Our platform is best-in-class for retention."
- ✓ "94 percent of accounts renewed, against 81 percent across the segment."

**Fine when** the evidence sits right there. "Best-in-class retention" is a claim, not slop, when the next line reads "94 percent renewal, against 81 percent for the segment." Judge the claim and its support together, not the adjective alone.

### Throat-clearing verbs

Bureaucratic scaffolding around a simple instruction.

- ✗ "Going forward, it would be prudent for the department to initiate an evaluation of the current vendor arrangement."
- ✓ "The department should evaluate the vendor contract before it renews in March."
- ✗ "Staff should be informed of the operational implications associated with this transition."
- ✓ "Tell staff the new process adds a supervisor check on refunds over $500."

**Quick habit:** try shortening it in your head. If you can't produce a shorter version that keeps everything, it wasn't this.

### Tone words

Vocabulary that shows up far more in generated prose than in human drafts. Frequently noted: *showcase, underscore, foster, robust, intricate, pivotal, delve, testament, vibrant, landscape.* The list isn't fixed and isn't a blocklist.

- ✗ "This intricate landscape underscores a pivotal moment, and the program's robust design is a testament to what a city can foster."
- ✓ "Ridership rose 11 percent after the route change, almost all of it on the two crosstown lines."

**Density is the signal.** One "robust" is a word choice. Seven of these in a sentence is a tell.

### Two-sided nothing

Balance performed without a tradeoff named, so the sentence can't be wrong.

- ✗ "While self-checkout speeds up transactions, it also presents unique challenges."
- ✓ "Self-checkout cut average wait by 90 seconds and raised shrink by 1.4 points."

A real tradeoff names both conditions and both outcomes, and could be disproven. Don't confuse this with a stripped condition, which fails for the opposite reason.

### Vague verb

A vague verb usually carries more of the damage than the abstract noun next to it. Watch *anchored to, oriented around, operationalizes, reads as, serves as.*

- ✗ "Event capacity remains anchored to dual constraints: an attendee cap and a percentage cap."
- ✓ "Events are limited by a 200-person cap and a 60 percent room-capacity rule."

### Weightless significance

Ordinary facts inflated into turning points.

- ✗ "This quarter's inventory write-down marks a pivotal moment in the retailer's evolving landscape."
- ✓ "The inventory write-down was $3.1M, most of it seasonal apparel."
- ✗ "The revised guideline represents a profound shift in how the department operates."
- ✓ "The revised guideline moves approval authority from the director to the shift lead."

### Wishful ending

A forward-looking paragraph that doesn't grow out of anything above it.

- ✗ "Looking ahead, the challenges are real, but the opportunity to build a lasting legacy of service remains."
- ✓ Delete, or state the actual next event: "The board votes on the renewal in October."

## Things that look like tells and aren't

Over-flagging costs as much as under-flagging. Leave these alone:

- **A distinction where both sides are observable.** "The exclusion covers frozen pipes, not roof leaks."
- **A warning.** "Don't run the update after 5 p.m.; overnight jobs will fail and can't be restarted."
- **A named source or a measured result.** "The March 4 inspection found 11 unlabeled containers."
- **A rule, limit, or sign-off.** "Legal and Safety must approve before the pilot starts." "The vendor may terminate only after 30 days' written notice."
- **A real term of art.** "The endpoint returns 409 when the record has already been updated."
- **A hedge with its reason attached.** "The estimate is preliminary because two sites haven't reported."
- **Punctuation doing structural work.** A colon before an actual list; one dash before real substance.
- **Scope and navigation.** "This memo covers the two vendor decisions due Friday." "Region 4 is excluded because its data missed the cutoff."
- **Sequence stated as sequence.** "Incidents fell 20 percent after the new screening" reports what happened without claiming cause. That's careful, not evasive.
- **Terse forms in the right container.** Semicolon imperatives inside a table cell are correct. The same shape in prose isn't.
- **Plain, short, confident prose.** Brevity isn't laziness and length isn't padding. Judge decisions per word.

## Reading a whole document

Sentence-level passes miss two things:

**The closing synthesis.** The most commonly flagged line in real documents is the summary sitting under genuinely good content, added to sound conclusive after the work was already done. Check it first.

**Repeated claims.** When the same point appears twice, once with evidence and once as an aphorism, the aphorism is the finding. A single maxim is arguable. A document that alternates substance and restatement is answering the question for you.

Also watch the ratio: a two-sentence answer split across six headings and twelve bullets is over-built, no matter how clean each sentence is.

## When you're the one writing

- Put a number, a name, a date, or a mechanism where the device wanted to go.
- Give passive constructions an actor.
- Prefer deletion to rewriting. Most of this catalog repairs by cutting the evaluative half and keeping nothing.
- Keep every load-bearing condition. If the shorter version can no longer be proven wrong, it's worse.
- Put the conclusion in the title or the first line.

> **Structure is not the finding. Absence of content is.**
